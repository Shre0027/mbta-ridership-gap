Who the turnstiles miss.

The MBTA publishes two official ridership numbers that disagree by up to 23% on the Blue Line. The MBTA's data team suspected growth in East Boston. Three independent datasets point somewhere else: riders changing trains underground, where no fare gate ever sees them.

The answer in 30 seconds

Riders who change lines underground, where no gate sees them

The monthly dataset undercounts the Blue Line by 20–23% every fall since 2023. The gap comes from riders who walk from the Orange or Green Line onto the Blue at State and Government Center. Fare taps can't see them; the trip model can.

  1. Only the transfer hubs are off. Four downtown stations show 2.8–5.1 modeled boardings per tap. 54 of 61 gated stations sit at 0.9–1.5×. See the map →
  2. Riders say so too. In the 2025 survey, 82% of Blue Line riders at Government Center and 60% at State arrived on another subway line. Taps plus those transfers land within 3% of the model. See the test →
  3. Nothing else explains it. It holds on weekends, under both date windows and with every shutdown day removed, and East Boston stations scale normally. See the stress tests →
The fix: recalibrate the Blue Line factor once a year; out of sample, the error falls from 18% to 6%. Recommendations →Still live: Fall 2026 early read →

01 · The problem

Same line, same season, two different answers.

The MBTA's Office of Performance Management & Innovation (OPMI) publishes both of these. Both are "official." They are built in different ways, for different jobs.

In February 2025 a developer asked on the MBTA's public forum why Fall 2023 Blue Line numbers differed by 23.4%. OPMI replied that the methods differ, named East Boston development as the likely cause, and said aligning the two is ongoing work.

02 · The whole system

Where the turnstiles can't see riders.

Before zooming in on the Blue Line, run the same test on every subway station. Divide the boardings the trip model counts by the fare taps at that station. Most stations land near 1.2×, the normal allowance for kids, free riders and people who don't tap. A few stations are far above that.

Modeled boardings ÷ gate taps, every subway station

Model ÷ taps:no fare gates (Green Line surface and GLX stops)

So what?The dark circles are all in one place: the downtown stations where two lines meet underground. Park Street (4.3×), Government Center (5.1×), Downtown Crossing (3.2×) and State (2.8×) are where riders change lines without passing a fare gate, and a count built from taps can't see those transfers. 54 of the 61 gated stations sit between 0.9× and 1.5×. South Station (1.9×) is the Silver Line connection behind the gates. Only Assembly (1.6×) and Wollaston (1.5×) are also above 1.5×. They aren't transfer stations, and both were at 1.0–1.05× before 2020, so they look like the far end of the post-COVID rise in riders who don't tap, not missed transfers.

So what?At the downtown hubs the gap is a morning story. At 8 AM Government Center counts about 14 modeled boardings for every tap, and Park Street, Downtown Crossing and State about 7–9. By 5 PM all four are down to about 2–3×. In the morning, commuters change trains downtown on their way in, without tapping. In the evening, many trips start downtown, so people tap in. Regular stations like Harvard and Maverick sit near 1× all day. Fare evasion wouldn't follow the commute like this. Transfers do.

04 · A third source

Riders say the same thing the data does.

The taps and the model both come from the fare system. To check them independently, I used a different source: the MBTA's 2025 System-Wide Passenger Survey (145,468 responses systemwide), which asks riders how they reached each train. At Government Center, 82% of Blue Line riders said they arrived on another subway line. At State it was 60%. At Maverick, Airport and Wonderland, it was 0–2%.

05 · The bridge

From one number to the other, line by line.

Divide the monthly Blue Line number by the Blue Line gate taps and you get the single factor it effectively applies to every tap: about 1.2–1.4×. With that, the gap splits exactly into two parts.

Bridge from Dataset A to Dataset B, Blue Line

Average weekday riders, Fall 2025

So what?Every year the model finds 18,000–26,000 more Blue Line riders at State and Government Center than the monthly factor credits there. The monthly method partly makes up for it by over-crediting regular stations, but never fully. That cushion was about 17,000 riders in 2019 but only 5,000–8,000 since 2023, so more of the hub shortfall shows up in the gap.

Method note: the bridge treats the monthly dataset as one factor applied to all Blue Line taps. OPMI's actual factors aren't published, so the split between the two parts is a model of their method, not their own breakdown.

06 · Over time

One real shift, and one silent swap.

I ran the same test for every season from 2017 to 2025. Two things changed. Neither one is flagged in the data, and only one of them helps explain the gap.

So what?Before COVID the model was within 3–7% of the taps. Since 2023 it adds 16–24% for riders who don't tap, likely using faregate passenger counters. A fixed factor in the monthly method wouldn't move with it, which fits the gap growing after 2019.

So what?On March 6, 2019, the tap data's split at Government Center flipped. It went from 90% Blue and 10% Green to 10% Blue and 90% Green, overnight, with no note anywhere. Anyone trending station entries across that date would see a ridership collapse that never happened.

So why do the two numbers disagree?

Behind-the-gate transfers downtown

About a third of Blue Line boardings happen at State and Government Center, mostly by riders who already tapped in on another line. The tap-based method has to estimate them with fixed factors. The trip model follows each trip.

A bigger allowance for non-tappers after COVID

The model's uplift at ordinary stations rose from about 1.05× to about 1.2×. A single fixed factor in the monthly method wouldn't move with it, which fits the gap roughly doubling after 2019.

And the Government Center swap? It changes the tap data, not the gap: the gap actually shrank from 2018 to 2019. It matters because it's an unflagged break in a public dataset.

And East Boston? Maverick, Airport, Wood Island, Orient Heights and the Revere stations scale steadily, at almost the same ratio as every other regular station. The data doesn't show them driving the gap.

And the Red Line's 2024 dip? That one is a shutdown, not a counting rule. The MBTA closed the Braintree branch for 24 days in September 2024 and part of the line again in November, with shuttle buses replacing trains. The monthly number fell in those months, to 124,470 in September and 106,335 in November. In October, the one fall month without a major closure, it was 146,264, within 1.3% of the model's 148,203.

Caveat: OPMI doesn't publish the exact factors behind Dataset A, so how much of the gap each reason explains is inferred from the published data, not confirmed by the MBTA.

Stress tests

Could anything else explain the gap?

Three checks, each with a different dataset: how much the two datasets disagree when nothing is wrong (the bus), whether shutdowns are behind it (the MBTA's alert archive), and whether it's still happening this fall (an early read on 2026).

Show the table and how the days were picked

A weekday counts as disrupted for a line when an alert with effect "shuttle" or "suspension" on that line is active across the morning peak (8–9 AM) and touches at least two of its stations. The archive also holds stale copies of old alerts that reappear months later with no edits; those are dropped. The monthly dataset is only published as monthly averages, so the undisrupted version uses each month's own factor (monthly ÷ taps) times gate taps on undisrupted days. The stop-level side can't be filtered by day, so treat the right-hand number as a sensitivity check.

An early read

What about this fall?

How the early read works, and how well it did before

For each line: last fall's model boardings ÷ last fall's gate taps, times this fall's gate taps, gives an expected model count. The monthly figure is compared against it, using September and October, the months that are published first. Run the same rule on every past fall that does have model data, and the expected gap misses the real one by the amount shown as the whisker (average miss, in points). It's a forecast, not a measurement, until the MBTA publishes the fall's stop-level data.

07 · And the Green Line?

The Green Line swing comes from the monthly factor, not the riders.

The Green Line gap flips sign: +24% in 2019, −16% in 2023, +15% in 2024. Many Green Line riders board at street-level stops with no fare gates, so the monthly number leans much harder on scaling. The question is whether the scaling holds steady.

So what?Across 2018–2026 the monthly Green factor wanders between about 1.7× and 3.1×, while Red, Orange and Blue stay mostly at 1.25–1.5×. The model's Green Line factor stays near 2.1–2.4× in 2018 and 2023–25. The monthly factor drops to mostly 1.7–2.0 from July 2023 to June 2024, then jumps to mostly 2.4–2.8 from August 2024. The single-month spikes (January 2024, December 2024, December 2025) are months when Green Line taps fell much faster than the monthly estimate. Fall 2023 lands in the low stretch (monthly below the model), and Fall 2024–25 in the high one (monthly above). Red, Orange and Blue barely move.

Show the table

What changed in August 2024? On August 1, 2024 the MBTA launched its new fare system ("Tap to Ride"), with contactless readers onboard Green Line trolleys and at gated stations. That is the month the monthly Green factor jumped. A later NBC10 Boston / Boston University investigation reported that the share of Green Line riders boarding at ungated stops rose from about 42–46% before the launch to 56.5% in September 2024. More riders boarding where there are no gates means more of the monthly number has to come from outside the gate taps, so the ratio rises. The timing lines up. OPMI hasn't published a method note confirming it.

One more break, on the other side. In Fall 2019 the stop-level data counts about half as many boardings at ungated surface stops as in 2018: 21,000 vs 44,000, or 15% of Green Line boardings instead of 25%. That's why the model factor dips to 1.8× that year and the 2019 gap looks so large. The data doesn't explain the change either.

Shutdowns move the Green Line's numbers too. In both 2024 and 2025 the MBTA closed the central Green Line subway for about two weeks in December (in 2025, no trains between North Station and Kenmore from Dec 8 to Dec 22). The monthly Green figure for December fell 16% below November in 2024 and 35% in 2025, while the Blue Line fell 11–15%. That's why the Green gap moves by as much as 12 points (2025) between the two date windows in the bus yardstick table above, against a typical 3–4 points for the other lines. Fall 2025 also had a nine-day core closure (Sep 26–Oct 4) and a Green Line Extension closure into mid-November. So read the Green Line's year-to-year gap as noisy. The factor jump in August 2024 is the steadier signal.

08 · Why it matters

A counting rule isn't just a technicality.

Federal reporting

Dataset A is the reporting feed

The monthly dataset is published as the MBTA's "NTD_Reporting" service. National Transit Database figures feed federal formula funding: FTA's Section 5307 urbanized-area formula uses NTD service data, including passenger miles. How riders are counted flows into what gets reported.

Planning

Two numbers, two pictures of the Blue Line

One dataset says about 50,000 Blue Line riders on a fall weekday, the other about 63,000. Service, crowding and investment decisions read very differently depending on which one a planner picks up.

Riders

Who rides the line that gets undercounted

In the 2025 rider survey, 67% of Blue Line riders were classified low-income under the MBTA's Title VI definition, and 54% as minority. Accurate counts matter most where service decisions affect riders who depend on transit.

Trust

Official numbers should reconcile

When two official sources disagree with no published explanation, every downstream user, from journalists to planners, has to guess which one to trust. A published bridge removes the guesswork.

This project doesn't estimate funding impact. NTD reporting uses its own annual procedures, and formula funding depends on many factors. The point is that the counting method matters, and should be documented.

Recalibrate

Refresh the Blue Line factor every fall

Calibrating the tap-based Blue Line number against the fall trip model once a year cut its error from 18% to about 6% in an out-of-sample test (chart above). It's one number per line per year, and it reuses data OPMI already produces.

Document

Publish the scaling factors and their history

List the non-interaction, station-split and transfer factors used in Dataset A, with the date each one changed. Users could then reconcile the datasets themselves.

Flag

Mark method changes in the data

Add a method-version field or a change note to every dataset, so breaks like the Government Center swap (March 2019), the Green Line surface count (2019) and the Green Line factor shift at the August 2024 fare-system launch aren't mistaken for real ridership changes.

Standardize

One name and ID per station and line

"State" vs "State Street," and a Green Line split into four branches in only one season. Shared IDs would make every join reliable.

Reconcile

Ship an official bridge table

Section 05 is my outside-in version. OPMI could publish the real one: for each line and season, taps plus each factor's contribution equals Dataset A, set beside Dataset B. The gap stops being a mystery and becomes a line item.

10 · Method

How this was built

  • Sources: MBTA Monthly Ridership by Mode and Line; Rail Ridership by Season, Route/Line and Stop (Fall 2017–2019, 2023, 2024, 2025, incl. by hour); Gated Station Entries (historical, 2017–2026); MBTA 2025 System-Wide Passenger Survey; MBTA Bus Ridership by Trip, Season, Route/Line and Stop (Fall 2016–2025); MBTA archived GTFS-Realtime service alerts (2020–2025); MBTA Ridership Guide (OPMI); official GTFS route shapes and stops; MassGIS town boundaries. All public.
  • Window: weekdays, September to December. Monthly figures are day-weighted, which reproduces the forum post's Fall 2023 numbers exactly.
  • Stop-level totals: the sum of average weekday boardings across every stop, direction and time period.
  • Station test: raw gate taps averaged over non-holiday weekdays, compared with modeled boardings at each station. Normal uplift (1.20×) = model ÷ taps at heavy-rail stations where under 5% of riders arrive by subway. Each station's taps are averaged over the days it was open, so shutdowns with shuttle buses don't count as zero-rider days.
  • Survey check: share of riders who arrived on another subway or light-rail line ("Access to Each MBTA Trip Leg"), weighted by modeled boardings across the lines at each station.
  • Map: official GTFS route shapes (one canonical shape per service), MassGIS town boundaries for land. Hero trains: every scheduled subway trip on one regular Tuesday from the MBTA GTFS feed, interpolated between scheduled stop times along each trip's own shape.
  • Data quality fixes: "State Street" mapped to "State"; Fall 2025 Green Line branches rolled up to one line; Green Line Extension stations treated as ungated; stale copies of old alerts removed from the alert archive. Sixteen issues are logged in the repo.
